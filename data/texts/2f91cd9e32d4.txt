i The Ruhi Institute is an educational institution functioning under the aegis of the National Spiritual
Assembly of the Bahá’ís of Colombia. Its purpose is to develop human resources dedicated to the
spiritual, social and cultural progress of the Colomb ian people. Over the decades its materials have
increasingly been used by national Bahá’í communities in all parts of the world.
The approach to curriculum development followed by the Ruhi Institute differs significantly from
the traditional one of design, field-testing and evalua tion, carried out in a linear fashion. The first
step in writing any set of materials is taken, rather, when an experience is created at the grassroots in performing some act of service in response to the exigencies of the development of a community.
Materials emerge out of this experience and become  an expression of it. They are, on the one hand,
a record of the learning that occurs in applying the teachings of the Bahá’í Faith in a particular area
of service and, on the other, an instrument for th e systematization of that learning. The approach
has been described in the follo wing terms in the small volume Learning About Growth:  The story
of the Ruhi Institute and large-scale expansion of the Bahá’í Faith in Colombia:
Once an educational need is identified, a sm all group of people, working at the grassroots,
consult among themselves, develop a set of ideas for educational activities, and put them into
practice. The results of this practice are review ed, evaluated and consulted upon; in light of
this consultation, a modified set of educationa l activities is put into practice, and subjected
to reflection that leads to further modificati on and revision. At no time in this process of
curriculum development does action await th e final preparation and evaluation of
educational materials. At every stage, educa tional activities proceed with the best materials
at hand, in the conviction that it is only throug h practice and reflection, both pursued in light
of the Revelation of Bahá’u’lláh,  that more appropriate curricu la can gradually evolve. Yet,
this is not a setting in which a few individuals  are developing materials for their own use,
and it does become necessary at  some point to finalize the st ructure and content of each unit
so that it may be used with confidence by othe rs. The decision to release a final version of
the materials for a given course is made simply  when it is noticed that modifications are
becoming negligible. It is important to note that the various aspects of this process of
consultation, action and reflection are carried  out parallel with one another, and not
performed in a linear sequence which would be inherently artificial.
As the use of the materials of the Ruhi Institu te has become more wid espread, the development
process has drawn increasingly on experience worldw ide in applying the teachings to the life of
humanity. Though in practice the process today can ta ke divers forms, depe nding on the nature of
the material in development, there are generally thre e stages that give rise to what is considered the
final published edition.  1. Initial Outline (IO)
At this stage a course or text contains the basi c concepts and a set of passages from the Bahá’í
writings that, taken together, are believed to ach ieve the educational objective in question. For
some time, this initial outline of ideas is used by small groups of individuals in the field, as part
of the efforts to elaborate effective course content.
2. Alpha Version  (AV)
As action progresses, the material is refined a nd begins to reflect new experience gained,
incorporating ideas that arise out of it. Not in frequently, certain concepts are amended, new
passages from the Bahá’í writings added, or a seri es of simple exercises introduced to facilitate


ii comprehension of a topic or to help develop an  important skill or attitude . Through this process,
a consolidated course or text emerges which is generally regarded as the “alpha version”.
3. Pre-Publication Edition  (PP)
In time it is determined, generally on the basis of th e integrity of the content, to make the course
or text more widely available as a pre-publicati on edition. Through its continued use, it becomes
apparent when a section needs to be moved or an exercise or two revised. Several successive
versions may appear. Gradually, however, fewe r and fewer modifications are required to
achieve the intended purpose of the course or text , and the material, at this stage, goes for
publication.
We are happy to make available to selected organizations and institu tions one of the units that has
reached the pre-publication stage. It is the second of three units of a book currently in development
entitled Family and the Community . You are encouraged to send us any comments you have on the
basis of your experience in using the course content.

iii NOTES FOR THE TUTOR
This is the second of three units currently in deve lopment by the Ruhi Institute that will make up
the twelfth book in its main series. It seeks to enab le participants to propa gate a conversation on
the education and upbringing of children among fa milies in villages and neighborhoods where the
processes of the expansion and consolidation of the Faith are gathering momentum and where a
sense of community life has emerged. The premi se underlying the unit is that, while parents are
primarily concerned with the sound development of their own children, they share a common
responsibility with every member of the community  for the well-being of all the youngsters in a
village or neighborhood.
It is envisioned that the study of the unit and the act of service to which it gives rise will
further advance in participants th e capabilities they have been devel oping ever since they entered the
institute process—that is, the capab ilities which enable them to engage  in meaningful and uplifting
conversation. What should be acknow ledged is that, by the time individuals reach this point in the
sequence of institute courses, they will have alr eady gained a good deal of experience in paying
visits to homes and in conversing with members of households on a variety of topics and themes,
including but not limited to those addressed in the second and third units of Book 2, as well as concepts embedded in the programs for the spiritu al education of children and for the spiritual
empowerment of junior youth. Such visits have b ecome as integral to the life of each participant,
then, as they have to the pattern of community lif e that distinguishes every center of intense activity.
The tutor should appreciate that this affects both the way the content is presented in the unit and
how participants are expected to make use of it.
The unit picks up the story of Alejandra from Book 2 and proposes that three years have
passed, during which the community-building effort s in her neighborhood have  intensified greatly.
Now a young mother, Alejandra decides with a sm all group of friends, including her husband, to
try to gain insights into what the Bahá’í t eachings say about the education and upbringing of
children, insights the group plans to share in conversation with others in the neighborhood,
especially with parents of the children and junior  youth who are participating in the educational
programs of the institute. The unit asks participants  first to deepen, as do Alejandra and her friends,
their own understanding of each of the themes thus a ddressed. To this end, they are encouraged to
reflect on certain relevant concepts in light of guidance enshrined in the writings and to consider
their implications for the education of children, aided by a set of corresponding exercises.
As should become apparent to the tutor, pa rticipants will have already encountered many
of the ideas and quotations presented in the unit in previous courses. Yet, their organization into
themes related to raising children w ill enable participants to draw fr esh insight into the meaning of
the quotations and will further enhance their understa nding of the implications of the teachings for
their own lives. It can well be imagined that t hose who study the unit will be predominantly young
people, either single or newly married, activ ely engaged in the processes of expansion and
consolidation and committed to the material and spiritual progress of their villages and neighbor-
hoods. The reflections they undertake in this a nd the preceding unit, concerned with Bahá’í
marriage, will serve as a means of preparation for them as they enter the st age of life where they
are ready to start forming families—an indispensab le element of the future civilization conceived
by Bahá’u’lláh. It would not be unreasonable to assume  that in the strivings of more and more such
young adults we will catch the first glimpses of a family life that conforms to His teachings as
depicted in these pages.


iv That said, though the content of the unit will serve to increase the understanding of
participants, this is by no means the primary objec tive. To share the gems of wisdom we discover
in the ocean of Bahá’u’lláh’s Revelation is a most profound source of joy. “With the hands of
renunciation draw forth from its life-giving waters,” are the words of Bahá’u’lláh studied in Book 2,
“and sprinkle therewith all created things, that th ey may be cleansed from all man-made limitations
and may approach the mighty seat of God, this hallowed and resplendent Spot.” Like Alejandra
and her companions, participants are encouraged to  initiate, concurrent with their study, a series of
visits to homes for the purpose of exploring passag es from the writings with families and interested
individuals in their village or neighborhood. It is envisaged that the kind of conversation promoted
by this act of service, in deepening understanding of concepts and principles fundamental to the
programs for the spiritual education of children a nd for the spiritual empowerment of junior youth,
will go far in reinforcing the aims of those program s. It is quite possible that, in some instances,
several families may decide to gather together regularly for the purpose of discussing the themes
addressed in the unit. Devotional meetings, commun ity gatherings and festivals, as well as certain
other activities held at the local level, might also  prove to be suitable spaces for this purpose. In
this unit, then, study and action run parallel to each other, and the tutor will be challenged to bring
to bear on the former the insights and experien ce being gained by participants in the latter.
It may be helpful to mention here that the un it is essentially organized into three parts. In
the first few sections, participants become familia r with the purpose of the unit, identify families
and individuals they might visit, either individua lly or in teams, and consider how they will go
about inviting them to take part in a series of conve rsations. They then explore, in the next several
sections, the broad subject of education, examini ng its various dimensions  and its importance to
the proper development of the human being. The unit continues with an exploration of the role of
the family in the emergence of a just and harm onious civilization, including how the family must
conduct itself and what should distinguish th e relationships that bind its members.
As to the nature of the conversation being prom oted, it is at once more structured and more
exploratory than those in which participants have engaged up to now. That the themes addressed
from visit to visit build on one another, to an extent, lends a degree of structure to the overall
conversation and to the direction in which it mov es. Yet, in the main, every discussion should be
viewed as an opportunity for all involved to exam ine the reality faced by families and to analyze
issues and concerns in light of the writings. To  undertake the kind of exploration envisioned,
participants are asked to consider the circumstan ces of families visited and determine how they will
draw on the content studied in each section to bu ild a conversation and which quotations from the
selection provided they will share. It is hoped that  the participants’ engagement with the themes,
including their deliberations on the exercises, will en able them to make sure the conversation they
facilitate flows between the abstract and the concrete—sometimes helping family members to
explore the practical application of an educationa l concept and at other times the spiritual principle
underlying a concrete course of action. What is requi red of participants reflects the degree to which
those who have reached Book 12 will have advan ced in the requisite capabilities—not only in the
amount of knowledge they have ac quired but also in the qualities, attitudes, and skills they are
developing.
Finally, it should be recognized that most of those who take part in discussions around the
themes treated in the unit will already have some knowledge of the Bahá’í teachings; yet it is quite
likely that many of them will not have had any previous opportunity to study systematically and
reflect on a set of quotations from the writings as described above. Thus the conversation should
be seen, above all, as a means of extending the edif ying influence of the Word of God to a wider


v circle of the population—to indivi duals who will, in turn, through th eir daily interactions, begin to
propagate even further afield ideas and concepts gleaned from the writings and who will contribute,
in this way, to the creation of an environm ent conducive to the spiritual and intellectual
development of children.

In the first unit of this book, we undertook an e xploration of the writings of the Faith that
shed light on the nature of Bahá’í marriage. There is no doubt that a sound understanding of the
principles involved lays a strong foundation upon wh ich a harmonious and healthy family life can
be built. Naturally, then, the next topic to which we will turn our attention is the raising of children.
Our deliberations will focus on the spiritual deve lopment of children within the home. It is
hoped, however, that the study of this unit and the actions to which it gives rise will contribute to a wider goal—that of a community assuming more a nd more responsibility for the education of all
its youngsters. To this end, it is possible to envision a conversation unf olding among many families
and residents of a given neighbor hood or village on this matter of great importance. How such a
conversation is initiated, promoted, and sustained is the central concern of this unit. In your study
of the second and third units of Book 2 and your s ubsequent participation in systematic programs
of visits to homes in your community, you have no doubt advanced in those capabilities needed to
enter into meaningful and uplifting conversation. This unit will assist you in further enhancing these
capabilities by familiarizing you with relevant t eachings on a number of interconnected themes
related to raising children, to be explored in a series of conversations with parents and others.

Each of us is a member of a family, and at the same time we are all members of a com-
munity. We are convinced that, at a fundament al level, the progress of one is inseparable
from the progress of the other. The followi ng passage written by the Universal House of
Justice seems to apply as much to the family unit as it does to other groups:
“The welfare of any segment of humanity is inextricably bound up with the welfare
of the whole. Humanity’s collective life suffers when any one group thinks of its own well-being in isolation from that of its neighbors . . .”
The family is not an isolated unit; it deri ves benefits from, and contributes to, the
development of the community. The House of Justice explains:
“And just as the viability of every cell and every organ is contingent upon the health
of the body as a whole, so should the pr osperity of every individual, every family,
every people be sought in the well-being of the entire human race.”10
Naturally, we each need to tend to the well-bei ng of our family. Moreover, every parent is
eager to create a loving, stable, and united e nvironment for the upbringing of children. Yet,
in thinking about this responsibility, we must heed the warning of the Universal House of
Justice that “excessive attention to family interests can lead, however unwittingly, to a
narrow social outlook, one that is ultimately detrimental to the broader community.” The
education children receive at home should help them gain a vision that is world-embracing.
The House of Justice states:
“. . . through education children gradually learn to look beyond their own interests to
those of their family. With yet further training, they recognize the importance of
respecting the interests of others and see as  a sacred obligation service to their
